The classic "missing dollar" riddle relies on a deliberately misleading equation. Here’s the flawed reasoning it typically presents:

1. Three travelers pay $10 each → **$30 total**
2. The room actually costs $25, so the clerk returns $5
3. The travelers keep $2 as a tip and return $1 each to the clerk
4. Each traveler now paid $9 → **$27 total paid**
5. **Flawed conclusion:** $27 (paid) + $2 (tip) = $29. Since they started with $30, **$1 is missing**.

### Precisely what's wrong:
The error is in **step 5**: adding $27 and $2 together is mathematically meaningless because **the $2 tip is already included in the $27**. 

- $27 = $25 (room cost) + $2 (tip)
- Adding the tip again **double-counts** it.

### Correct accounting:
- **Initial cash:** $30
- **Returned to travelers:** $3
- **Actually paid to hotel:** $27
- **Breakdown of $27:** $25 (room) + $2 (tip)
- **Balanced equation:** $30 = $27 (kept by hotel) + $3 (returned to travelers)

The "missing dollar" is a cognitive illusion created by mixing two different accounting frames: one tracking what was *returned* vs. *kept*, and another tracking room cost vs. tip. No dollar vanishes; the riddle's trick is in the invalid addition.
